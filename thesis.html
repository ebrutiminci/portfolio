Feeling at Home in the CityAn emotional map of Ljubljana's streets

What makes a city feel like home? I built a research app that asked people how they felt, in the moment, in Ljubljana's streets.

"Public space is not a static backdrop." From my thesis

Plan

Why an app, for an architecture thesis

A typical thesis would have designed a building or a square. I wanted to know how people already experience the city before proposing anything, and a one-time survey can't catch how a feeling changes from day to day. So I used the Experience Sampling Method (ESM): short, repeated check-ins in people's own surroundings, tagged with their location.

  1. Tell us about you: a one-time survey on age, gender, occupation and how long people have lived in Ljubljana.
  2. Tell us about Ljubljana: a one-time survey rating belonging, identity, comfort, safety and whether the city feels like home.
  3. Repeated check-ins: pick an emotion from Plutchik's wheel ("no emotion" is a valid answer), then answer three safety questions: how safe do you feel here, have you experienced or witnessed safety concerns here, and might people of a different gender feel differently?

To keep people coming back, the app keeps a streak, and an illustrated city grows each time someone takes part.

Research

Who took part

31 people took part and gave 98 responses with location data. Most were university students, from 11 different backgrounds: 20 women, 10 men and 1 who preferred not to say. 21 live in Ljubljana full time and 10 were there temporarily. On average they were 25 years old and had lived in the city for almost 8 years.

Limit, in my thesis's words: the sample does not represent a broader demographic. It is young and mostly students, so these are counts, not proof.

Synthesize

What people felt

  • 71 of 98daily check-ins named a positive emotion. 23 were negative and 4 were "no emotion".
  • Joywas picked most (17 times), then optimism (16) and serenity (11).
  • 0.18the link between feeling safe and feeling at home, the weakest in the study.

The clearest finding was a surprise. Feeling safe went together with feeling familiar and comfortable (0.62), but it barely connected to whether the city feels like home (0.18). What did connect to home was belonging and emotional connection. For design, that means a safe street isn't automatically a street people feel attached to. City identity scored lowest of all (mean 2.13) and varied the most between people.

Synthesize

Who feels safe, and who feels at home

  • 23 of 32responses from men said "very safe". Women chose "neutral" or "unsafe" more often.
  • 6 of 6"unsafe" responses came from full-time residents. No temporary resident felt unsafe.
  • 4emotions covered all 35 answers from full-time residents: optimism, joy, boredom and annoyance.

Temporary residents felt a much wider range of emotions, and every "anticipation" came from them. People who live in the city for good know it better, and feel it more narrowly. Safety also turned out to be a perception: some people felt safe despite past concerns, and some felt unsafe without any.

Synthesize · spatial

Mapping feelings onto the city

Every check-in carried a location, so I could put feelings on a map. Between 18 and 26 March 2025, 26 of the 31 participants sent 98 check-ins. I took the data from my own app to finished maps in four steps:

  1. 01 · AppCollectCheck-ins with emotion, safety answers and location
  2. 02 · ExcelCleanRearranged and recoded with formulas; each emotion given an intensity value
  3. 03 · QGISMapImported as points, colour-coded, turned into Voronoi polygons by frequency
  4. 04 · AdobeRefineFinal styling so the maps read at a glance

Emotion and safety overlapped most in the city centre. Part of that is simply more check-ins there, and part is the density of the centre itself. The maps then helped me choose the pilot site for the design.

What I learned the hard way: my first pass read the safety answers the wrong way round, because I didn't yet know how to read the raw export. Once I learned to check the data against the questions, I recoded it, and every number on this page comes from the corrected file. Also, IDW interpolation, thin plate spline and contour tools all failed in QGIS, so I switched to Voronoi polygons. The raw data stays private, because it holds participants' locations.

Design

From research to a design toolbox

The findings shaped a pilot design for Krakovo, one of Ljubljana's oldest neighbourhoods. Known as the city's garden on maps from the 16th century, it has been neglected recently. Instead of a fixed design, I proposed a toolbox of five steps, each turning something I found into something to design:

  1. Existing rhythm → movement
  2. Boundaries → informal space
  3. Local knowledge → surprise
  4. Research → process design
  5. Genius loci → context-specific design

The idea is a circular, participatory process: from object to process, from product to participation. That's the same loop I now use for digital products.

General notes

What it taught me

"Public space is not a static backdrop but a dynamic arena shaped by memory, rhythm, interaction, and design intent." From my thesis

This project is where architecture turned into product design for me. I had to plan a study, design an app people would open every day, keep them coming back, and then turn their answers into something a designer can act on. I'd do one thing differently now: recruit beyond my own circle, so the sample isn't mostly students.
